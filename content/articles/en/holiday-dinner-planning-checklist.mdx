Most holiday dinners don't go wrong because of a bad recipe. They go wrong because four dishes need the oven at the same time, the turkey is still half frozen on Wednesday night, and someone has to run to the store for butter an hour before guests arrive.

The fix is not more effort. It's planning backwards from the moment you serve, and keeping every recipe, quantity and shopping item in one place.

This checklist works for Thanksgiving, Christmas, Friendsgiving or any big dinner where you cook more than usual.

## Step 1: Lock the menu early (and keep it short)

Pick the menu about two weeks out. A calm holiday menu usually has:

- **one main** (turkey, roast, ham or a big vegetarian centerpiece)
- **two or three sides** that use different cooking methods
- **one make-ahead dessert**
- **one thing guests can bring**

The "different cooking methods" part matters more than it sounds. If the mashed potatoes are on the stove, the green beans are blanched ahead and the stuffing is in the oven, nothing fights for space. Three casseroles that all need 375°F for 45 minutes is how timelines fall apart.

Write the menu down with a link to every recipe, not just its name. "Grandma's stuffing" only helps if you can still find the actual quantities on the day.

## Step 2: Do the quantity math once

Scale recipes before you shop, not while you cook. Common rules of thumb:

| Dish | Plan per adult |
| --- | --- |
| Whole turkey (bone-in) | 1 to 1.5 lb |
| Boneless roast | about 1/2 lb |
| Mashed potatoes | 1/2 lb raw potatoes |
| Other sides | about 1/2 cup each |
| Gravy | 1/3 cup |
| Pie | 1 pie per 6 to 8 guests |

Round up slightly if you want leftovers. Then multiply each recipe so its ingredient list matches your guest count. That scaled list is what goes into the shopping list.

## Step 3: Build one shopping list, split by when you buy

Merge the ingredients from every recipe into one list, then combine duplicates. Five recipes that each call for "2 tbsp butter" add up faster than you expect.

Split the list into three buckets:

1. **Pantry and frozen (buy 10 to 14 days out):** flour, sugar, broth, canned goods, spices, frozen turkey, wine.
2. **Sturdy produce and dairy (buy 4 to 5 days out):** potatoes, onions, carrots, squash, butter, cream, cheese.
3. **Fresh and delicate (buy 1 to 2 days out):** herbs, salad greens, fresh bread, berries.

Before you shop, check what you already have. Half-used spice jars from last year are a classic way to buy cinnamon three times.

## Step 4: Count back from serving time

Pick the time you want to eat, then work backwards. Here's a realistic countdown for a dinner on a Thursday.

### Two weeks before
- Finalize the menu and guest count
- Scale recipes and build the shopping list
- Ask guests what they're bringing
- Check that you own a roasting pan, thermometer and enough serving dishes

### One week before
- Buy pantry items and a frozen turkey if you're using one
- Make and freeze anything that freezes well: pie dough, rolls, cranberry sauce, soup

### Thaw time (plan this one carefully)
A frozen turkey needs time in the refrigerator. The USDA recommends about **24 hours for every 4 to 5 pounds**. A 16 lb bird needs roughly four days, which means it goes into the fridge on Sunday for a Thursday dinner. Put it on a tray on the bottom shelf.

### Two days before
- Buy fresh produce
- Make dessert
- Chop sturdy vegetables and store them in containers
- Write the oven schedule (see Step 5)

### The day before
- Assemble casseroles and stuffing, cover and refrigerate
- Make gravy base from stock
- Set the table and label serving dishes with sticky notes
- Clear space in the fridge

### The day itself
- Take the main out of the fridge about an hour before roasting
- Roast the main
- While the main rests (30 to 45 minutes for a turkey), bake the sides
- Finish gravy and anything on the stovetop last

## Step 5: Write an oven schedule

The oven is the real bottleneck. Write a simple timeline on paper or in your notes:

| Time | Oven | Stove | Other |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 11:00 | Turkey in | | Set out appetizers |
| 2:30 | Turkey out to rest | Potatoes on | |
| 2:40 | Stuffing + casserole in | Green beans | Carve |
| 3:15 | Rolls in | Gravy | |
| 3:30 | | | Serve |

Use the resting time of the main dish as your sides window. It's the most useful 30 minutes of the day.

## Step 6: Plan the leftovers before you have them

Leftovers are part of the meal plan, not an afterthought. Keep containers and freezer bags ready, and refrigerate food within **two hours** of serving. The USDA suggests eating refrigerated leftovers within **three to four days** or freezing them.

Pick two "leftover dinners" for the days after the holiday now: turkey soup, sandwiches, a pot pie. Then you won't need another big shopping trip right after the big shopping trip. Our guide on [how to use up leftovers](/en/blog/articles/how-to-use-up-leftovers/) has a simple system for that week.

## FAQ

### How far ahead should I start planning a holiday dinner?
Two weeks is comfortable. That leaves time to finalize the guest list, buy pantry items without crowds and make freezer-friendly dishes ahead.

### How much turkey do I need per person?
Plan about 1 to 1.5 pounds of whole, bone-in turkey per adult. Go to the higher end if you want leftovers.

### What can I make the day before?
Casseroles, stuffing (baked separately from the bird), cranberry sauce, gravy base, pies and most desserts. Chopped vegetables also keep well for a day or two.

### How do I keep side dishes warm?
Bake them while the main rests, keep mashed potatoes in a slow cooker on "warm," and warm plates and serving dishes briefly in the oven once it's off.
